Search

What actually gets a local business found on Google

Local search is won on clarity, proximity and proof, not on keyword density. These are the changes that move a small business site, in the order I make them.

By Manoj Kumar Published 30 September 2026 5 min read

Short answer

Give each service its own page with a clear title, keep your Google Business Profile complete and identical to the site, make the pages fast and crawlable, and earn a few real reviews. Google documents no special markup for AI answers: a page that is indexed, clear and quotable is what gets cited.

Start with the Google Business Profile

For a business that serves a place, the map result usually comes before the website. The profile needs the exact trading name, the right primary category, real opening hours, the service area you can actually reach, and photos of the work. The name, address and phone number must match the site word for word. Inconsistent details split the signal and look sloppy to a customer.

Ask customers for reviews with a plain link, and reply to every one. Never offer rewards for positive reviews and never write them. Google's policies are clear on both, and a suspicious spike does more damage than a slow trickle.

One page per service

A page titled "Roof repair in New Delhi" can rank for that. A page titled "Services" listing eight trades competes with itself and gives the reader nothing specific. Split the services, then link between them with descriptive anchors: "roof repair" is a better link than "click here".

Do not create pages for towns you do not serve. Thin pages with a swapped city name are the classic doorway pattern and they do not survive scrutiny. One real page per service, plus a location page only where you have genuine work to show.

Titles and descriptions

  • The title is the blue line in results. Put the topic first, the brand last, and keep it around 50 to 60 characters.
  • The meta description is not a ranking factor, but it is your advert. Write 120 to 160 characters that make the click worth making.
  • One h1 per page, matching the topic. Then h2s as the questions people ask.
  • Every page needs a self-referencing canonical URL, using the https version you actually want indexed.

Technical basics that still decide everything

  1. Serve the text in the initial HTML, not after a script runs.
  2. HTTPS everywhere, one host version, no redirect chains.
  3. A sitemap.xml listing canonical URLs only, and a robots.txt that points to it.
  4. Fast pages: see the speed guide.
  5. Structured data matching what is visible on the page, validated with the Rich Results Test.

What AI answer engines need

Google states that AI Overviews and AI Mode need no special markup or file beyond being indexed and eligible for a snippet. There is no switch to flip. What helps is the same thing that helps a human: clear entity details (who, what, where, for whom), short self-contained answers, tables and step lists, dates on facts, and pages that load.

Retrieval crawlers such as OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot and Claude-SearchBot should be allowed in robots.txt if you want to be quotable. Training crawlers are a separate decision for the owner. An llms.txt file is optional: it has no measured effect on citations, so do not treat it as a ranking tactic.

Bing matters more than its market share suggests, because several AI products draw on its index. Add the site to Bing Webmaster Tools and use IndexNow for quick updates.

Measure, then wait

Search Console shows impressions, clicks, average position and which pages are indexed. Check it monthly and fix what the data shows: pages that get impressions but no clicks usually need a better title; pages that are not indexed usually need to be linked.

Indexing takes days to weeks. Competitive terms take months. Anyone promising a position by a date is guessing, and the sites that win are usually the ones that kept answering real questions while everyone else bought links.
