Platforms

WordPress, Wix or custom code: how to choose a website platform

All three can produce a good-looking site. They differ in who can change it, what it costs every year, and what happens when something breaks.

By Manoj Kumar Published 14 July 2026 4 min read

Short answer

Choose a page builder if you need to edit everything yourself and can accept a monthly fee. Choose WordPress if you need its plugin ecosystem and a familiar admin. Choose hand-coded HTML if speed, control and low maintenance matter more than editing every word yourself. Pick based on who maintains the site for the next three years, not on which demo looked best.

Start with one question: who edits the site?

A platform is a maintenance decision disguised as a design decision. If you will change prices, photos and opening hours yourself every month, the platform must have a friendly editor. If a developer or a family member handles changes, editing comfort matters far less than loading speed, security and long-term cost.

Most local businesses overestimate how often they will edit their own site. Be honest: if last year's Facebook page has three posts, a full content management system is probably an expensive way to write nothing.

Page builders: Wix, Squarespace, Shopify

Fastest route from nothing to a live site, and the editing experience is genuinely good. The trade-offs are real: a subscription for as long as the site exists, performance you cannot fully control, and export options that range from limited to non-existent. Moving a settled business off a builder usually means rebuilding.

  • Good for: a first site, a shop with a small catalogue, anyone who wants to change text without help.
  • Poor for: tight performance budgets, unusual layouts, long-term cost control.
  • Ask before starting: can I export the content, and what happens to the site if I stop paying?

WordPress

WordPress runs a large share of the web for a reason: it is flexible, well documented and most developers know it. The cost is maintenance. Themes and plugins need updates, updates occasionally break layouts, and an unmaintained WordPress site is a common route to a hacked domain. You need hosting with PHP and a database, plus someone willing to look at it every few months.

  • Good for: a site with many editors, a real blog cadence, or a business that needs niche plugins.
  • Poor for: anyone who wants zero maintenance, and small sites where speed is the priority.

Hand-coded static HTML

The site is a set of HTML and CSS files served from a CDN. There is no database to attack, no plugins to update and nothing to break on a Tuesday morning. Pages load fast because there is very little to load. The trade-off: changing text means editing a file, unless a small content setup is added to the scope.

This is what I build, and it is what this site is: plain HTML, two self-hosted fonts and no framework. Forms post to a hosted endpoint, so there is still no server to maintain. See what a build includes.

What I would tell a friend

If the business is under a year old and the owner wants to experiment, a builder is a reasonable place to start and easy to replace later. If the business has been running for years and the site is a sales tool, a static build is cheaper to own and faster to load. WordPress sits in the middle and is worth it when a real content operation exists.

None of these choices fixes bad content. A fast site with vague sentences still loses to a slow site that answers the customer's question. The next guide covers that: how to write website copy that gets a call.
