Mobile-first design: what it means for a small business site

Most visitors arrive on a phone, often on mobile data, often in a hurry. Designing the phone layout first is not a preference, it is the realistic starting point.

By Manoj Kumar Published 20 October 2026 4 min read

Short answer

Design the narrow layout first, at 360px wide: one column, body text at least 16px, tap targets at least 44px, no horizontal scrolling and the main action reachable with a thumb. Then add the wider layouts as enhancements. Starting from the desktop and shrinking tends to produce tiny text and crowded rows.

What mobile-first actually means

It means the phone layout is the design, not the fallback. You decide the order of content at 360px, where only one thing fits at a time. That forces a real hierarchy: what does the visitor need first, second and third? Wider screens then get the same content with more room, which is much easier than squeezing a desktop layout down.

The 360px test

  1. Open the page in a browser at 360px wide. (DevTools, or just use a phone.)
  2. Scroll to the bottom. If anything causes a sideways scroll, the layout fails.
  3. Read the first screen. Can you tell what the business does, where, and what to do next?
  4. Tap every link with a thumb. Anything smaller than 44px is a miss.
  5. Fill in the contact form with one hand, including the keyboard covering half the screen.

Sizing rules that prevent most problems

Minimums worth designing to
ElementMinimumNote
Body text16pxAlso stops iOS zooming into form fields
Tap target44 × 44pxHeight matters more than width for links
Contrast4.5:1Body text against its background
Line length30 to 45 charactersNarrower than desktop, still readable

Keep the action under the thumb

The top of a modern phone is for reading; the bottom is for acting. A call or booking button that repeats after the main sections, or a small sticky bar at the bottom of the screen, converts better than a single button at the top of a long page. If you add a sticky bar, leave room so it never covers the footer content.

Forms on phones

  • One column. Two columns of fields at 360px produce cramped boxes and mistyped emails.
  • Labels above the field, always visible. Placeholder text disappears as soon as typing starts.
  • The right keyboard: type="email", type="tel", and autocomplete on names and addresses.
  • Errors next to the field, in words, plus a focus move to the first problem.
  • No required fields you do not actually need. Every extra field costs submissions.

What usually breaks

Wide tables, fixed-width images, hover-only menus, small text in buttons, and viewport settings that block zooming. Never use user-scalable=no: it takes away the one tool a visitor with poor eyesight relies on.

Images are the other common failure. A hero sized for a laptop is wasted data on a phone, so serve a smaller file with srcset and set width and height to reserve the space. Speed and layout stability are part of mobile design, covered in the speed guide.

Desktop is the enhancement

Once the phone layout works, widen it: add a second column for supporting detail, move navigation into a horizontal bar, and let images breathe. The content order stays the same, which is why a mobile-first site rarely has the "desktop only" sections that nobody sees on a phone.
